Did generative AI hit female-dominated jobs harder?

Reed Marthers & Yashita SinghU.S. occupations, 2015–2025Results as of Nov 7, 2026
Short answer

No evidence they fared worse. After 2022, growth in high-exposure female-dominated occupations moved up slightly. Growth in high-exposure male-dominated occupations slowed. The difference favors female-dominated jobs, but it is not statistically significant, and it does not survive every check.

This is a descriptive comparison, not a causal estimate. The results come from the rebuilt analysis in the project repository (309 occupations), not from the original scripts.

Growth before and after 2022

The high-exposure female-dominated group is mostly clerical work: secretaries, office clerks, receptionists, bookkeeping clerks. It was already shrinking before 2022. The biggest slowdown was in high-exposure male-dominated jobs, led by software developers, computer support and computer systems managers.

Across the high-exposure occupations, the female-dominated group's change in growth was 2.45 points per year better than the male-dominated group's. The 95% interval runs from −1.70 to +6.60, so the data cannot rule out no difference.

Checks

Each check targets a specific weakness, with a pass standard written before the check was run on the rebuilt data. A failed check is reported as a finding.

Weak point

Pass standard

Result
Gap −0.06 pp/yr (SE 2.22, 95% CI −4.48 to +4.37). Only 12 female-dominated occupations count as high exposure under human ratings. The gap is essentially zero, so it fails the "stays positive" part, but nothing points to female-dominated jobs doing worse.

9,371k in 2019, 8,774k in 2022, 8,792k in 2025: −6.2% below 2019. Secretaries alone fell 807k. Without them, the rest of the group grew 3.4% (6,683k to 6,911k), but that is a diagnostic, not the standard.

Rival explanations and limits

  • Routine office work was already declining. Cortes et al. (2014) document routine jobs shrinking for decades, long before generative AI. The secretaries trend fits that pattern.
  • The tech slowdown. Software job postings had already fallen sharply by ChatGPT's launch (Indeed via FRED), which could explain the male-dominated slowdown without AI.
  • Small groups. Each high-exposure group has only 19–21 occupations, so single occupations move the totals.
  • No age breakdown here. The Canaries study found its effect among workers aged 22–25. This table-level data cannot test that.
  • Code changes. Two male-dominated tech occupations do not line up across the 2020 code change, so 2019 vs. 2025 levels are only compared for the female-dominated group.

Method and data

  • BLS CPS Table 11 annual averages for 2015, 2019, 2022, 2024 and 2025: employment and % women by occupation.
  • Census 2018 occupation code list and 2010→2018 crosswalk, used to build comparable occupation units across the 2020 code change.
  • Eloundou et al. (2024) GPT-4 β exposure scores, averaged from O*NET occupations up to Census codes.
  • Growth is compound annual growth between endpoint years. The gap comes from a regression of each occupation's change in growth on its gender group, among high-exposure occupations, weighted by 2022 employment, with robust standard errors.
