# Literature Review: AI Workforce Policy and Gender-Segregated Occupations

**Date:** 2026-11-13
**Query:** AI workforce policy and gender-segregated occupations, starting from Manning et al. (2026) and Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen (2025)

## Summary

Three bodies of work meet on this topic: exposure studies that ask which occupations generative AI could affect, early employment studies that ask whether anything has happened yet, and a newer policy literature that asks who would struggle most if displacement comes. The exposure studies agree that women are over-represented in highly exposed jobs, and they agree on why: clerical and administrative work. The ILO's March 2026 brief finds 29% of female-dominated occupations exposed against 16% of male-dominated ones. Manning and Aguirre (2026) add the policy-relevant twist. Exposure and adaptive capacity are positively correlated overall, so most exposed workers are well placed to adjust. The 6.1 million who are both highly exposed and low in adaptive capacity are about 86% women, concentrated in clerical roles.

The employment evidence is less settled and mostly not gender-specific. Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen (2025; updated August 2026) find a 19% relative shortfall in employment for 22–25-year-olds in exposed occupations in ADP payroll data, working through reduced hiring. Two other datasets, résumés (Hosseini & Lichtinger) and the CPS (Dominski, Boes & Lee), show similar declines for young workers. CPS studies of all ages (Gimbel et al. at Yale; Eckhardt & Goldschlag at EIG) find little or no aggregate relationship between exposure and employment, and Danish register data (Humlum & Vestergaard) rule out earnings or hours effects above about 1%. On gender specifically, there are two realized-outcome sources so far, and neither is peer-reviewed.
- A July 2026 update to the Stanford–ADP Canaries dashboard reports slower employment growth for young women than young men at every exposure level. The researchers attribute the gap to occupational composition rather than to AI exposure itself. It has only been reported by Fortune, not released as a paper.
- A Pew Research Center analysis of the CPS (October 2026) finds men's employment grew more than women's in more AI-exposed occupations, especially among workers without a college degree. Pew does not claim the gap is caused by AI.

Policy work trails both. The U.S. Department of Labor has folded AI into existing programs, including an AI literacy framework (February 2026), AI-focused Registered Apprenticeship resources (April 2026) and "Rapid Reskill" National Dislocated Worker Grants (June 2026). A Congressional Research Service note observes that the authorizing statutes don't mention AI. None of the programs we found targets women or clerical workers by name, which is the gap Manning et al.'s adaptive-capacity measure is designed to fill.

## Key Papers

### Manning & Aguirre (2026); Manning, Aguirre, Muro & Methkupally (2026) — Adaptive capacity to AI displacement
- **Main contribution:** Builds an occupation-level adaptive-capacity index (net liquid wealth, growth-weighted skill transferability, geographic density, share aged 55+) and crosses it with AI exposure.
- **Method:** Combines SIPP, ACS, OEWS, BLS projections, Lightcast, O*NET and Eloundou et al. (2024) exposure; 356 occupations covering 95.9% of U.S. employment. NBER WP 34705; companion Brookings article (January 21, 2026). The NBER book-chapter version reports different coverage (300 occupations, 91.9% of the workforce, 34.7 million exposed), so cite the version whose numbers you use.
- **Key finding:** 37.1 million workers are in the top exposure quartile, 26.5 million of whom have above-median adaptive capacity. 6.1 million have high exposure and low capacity, and about 86% of them are women, mostly in clerical and administrative roles.
- **Relevance:** This is the policy anchor for your project. It explains why a gender gap could matter for welfare even if employment effects are small, and it proposes using adaptive capacity to target workforce-adjustment funding.

### Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen (2025; rev. Aug 2026) — Canaries in the Coal Mine?
- **Main contribution:** Six descriptive facts on AI and employment from high-frequency payroll data.
- **Method:** ADP payroll records, Eloundou et al. exposure quintiles, comparisons by age with firm-time controls. Working paper, Stanford Digital Economy Lab.
- **Key finding:** No economy-wide displacement. Employment of 22–25-year-olds in highly exposed occupations is about 19% below its counterfactual as of June 2026 (15% at the July 2025 data vintage; the 2025 paper reported 13–16%). The adjustment works through hiring rather than separations, and it is concentrated where AI use automates rather than augments. The result holds after excluding tech firms and computer occupations and after controlling for interest-rate exposure. The authors note some pre-trends and say the estimates are not causal.
- **Relevance:** This is the age analogue of your gender question. It also notes that "women face greater AI exposure on average" but reports no gender breakdown.

### Stanford Digital Economy Lab & ADP Research, Canaries Dashboard (July 2026; reported in Fortune)
- **Main contribution:** The first gender split of the Canaries data.
- **Method:** The same ADP data, ages 22–25, by exposure quintile. Not yet a paper; reported by Fortune on July 22, 2026, in "Exclusive: Stanford and ADP have new payroll data on Gen Z women losing ground to men in the job market—and AI isn't the main culprit."
- **Key finding:** 43.8% of young women vs 32.4% of young men work in the most-exposed quintile. Women grew more slowly than men at every exposure level: +1.3% vs +2.7% a year in the least-exposed quintile, and −4.5% vs −2.5% in the most-exposed. The gap is about the same size at both ends (1.4 vs 2.0 points). The researchers say the differences are "driven primarily by occupational composition" and are not noticeably correlated with AI exposure. The live dashboard (updated October 21, 2026) states the men's figure as a contraction of 2.5% a year. It also shows past-year changes in the most-exposed group of −5.4% for women and −3.1% for men.
- **Relevance:** This is the closest existing result to your main claim, and it points the same way: gender gaps exist, but they don't track exposure. It isn't a paper yet, so cite the dashboard for the numbers and Fortune for the researchers' interpretation.

### ILO (2026) — Gen AI, occupational segregation and gender equality in the world of work
- **Main contribution:** Links generative-AI exposure to occupational segregation by sex.
- **Method:** Research brief using the ILO–NASK exposure index applied to occupations classified by gender composition.
- **Key finding:** 29% of female-dominated occupations are exposed vs 16% of male-dominated ones. The brief attributes the gap to clerical, administrative and business-support work.
- **Relevance:** This is the motivating fact for your project, and your milestone cites it as "ILO (Mar 2026)," which matches the ILO page (March 5, 2026).

### Gmyrek, Berg, Kamiński et al. (2025) — Generative AI and Jobs: A Refined Global Index of Occupational Exposure
- **Main contribution:** A task-level global exposure index (ILO with Poland's NASK). ILO Working Paper 140, May 2025, DOI 10.54394/HETP0387.
- **Method:** Uses the ISCO-08 task structure, with exposure scored by LLM and human validation.
- **Key finding:** About 25% of global employment is potentially exposed (34% in high-income countries). 4.7% of women's jobs are in the highest-exposure category vs 2.4% of men's, and 9.6% vs 3.5% in high-income countries.
- **Relevance:** This is the exposure measure behind the ILO gender brief, so the two ILO sources are not independent.

### Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin & Rock (2024) — GPTs are GPTs
- **Main contribution:** The standard U.S. occupation-level LLM exposure measure.
- **Method:** Human and GPT-4 ratings of O*NET tasks. *Science* 384, 1306.
- **Key finding:** Most U.S. workers have at least some tasks exposed, and exposure rises with wages.
- **Relevance:** This is your exposure measure, and it is also the one used by Canaries and Manning & Aguirre. Shared measurement means these studies are not independent on exposure.

### Hosseini & Lichtinger (2025; rev. May 2026) — Generative AI as Seniority-Biased Technological Change
- **Main contribution:** Firm-level evidence that GenAI adoption cuts junior hiring.
- **Method:** Résumé data on about 65–66 million workers at more than 280,000 firms, 2015–2025 (May 2026 revision; the October 2025 version reported about 62 million workers). Adoption is identified from "GenAI integrator" job postings, with difference-in-differences and triple-difference designs from 2023Q1. Working paper (SSRN 5425555).
- **Key finding:** Starting in 2023Q1, junior employment at adopting firms falls relative to non-adopters, reaching about 9% after six quarters. The decline comes through slower hiring, while senior employment is roughly flat.
- **Relevance:** This is independent-data support for the Canaries age result. It has no gender analysis, which is an obvious extension.

### Dominski, Boes & Lee (2025; v1 July 2025, v3 November 10, 2026) — Advancing AI Capabilities and Evolving Labor Outcomes
- **Main contribution:** Links changes in AI capability to CPS outcomes, with a 2017–22 placebo period.
- **Method:** Occupational exposure built from capability stages; CPS January 2023–June 2026; first-difference and fixed-effects designs. arXiv 2507.08244.
- **Key finding:** No change in employment or unemployment for occupations with larger increases in exposure. However, the number of workers under 30 falls about 8% per standard deviation of the change in exposure, through reduced entry.
- **Relevance:** It uses the same CPS universe as your IPUMS work and shows the CPS can detect age effects. Sex enters only as a control; heterogeneity is reported by age and education, not by sex.

### Gimbel, Kinder, Kendall & Lee (2025) — Evaluating the Impact of AI on the Labor Market (Yale Budget Lab)
- **Main contribution:** Asks whether the U.S. occupational mix is shifting faster since ChatGPT than in earlier technology waves.
- **Method:** CPS dissimilarity index, compared with the PC and internet eras.
- **Key finding:** The mix is changing slightly faster than in the past, but the shift predates generative AI. The authors' companion Brookings piece (same day) adds that the share of workers in high-, medium- and low-exposure jobs "has remained remarkably steady" since ChatGPT's release in November 2022.
- **Relevance:** This is the pre-trend rival in your design, and it supports your finding that post-2022 HEF/HEM gaps sit on the earlier trend.

### Eckhardt & Goldschlag (2025) — AI and Jobs: The Final Word (Until the Next One) (EIG)
- **Main contribution:** Tests several exposure measures against CPS unemployment and occupation switching.
- **Method:** CPS, five exposure measures (including Felten–Raj–Seamans AIOE), 2022 to early 2025.
- **Key finding:** Unemployment rose less in the most-exposed quintile (+0.30 pp) than the least-exposed (+0.94 pp), and there is no rise in occupation switching.
- **Relevance:** This is a null result on aggregate employment, and it also shows the result depends on which exposure measure is used, which relates to your Check 1 sensitivity.

### Humlum & Vestergaard (2025) — Large Language Models, Small Labor Market Effects
- **Main contribution:** The first worker-level evidence on chatbot adoption and earnings.
- **Method:** Danish adoption surveys (about 25,000 workers in 11 exposed occupations) linked to employer-employee registers; difference-in-differences using employer policies. NBER WP 33777. The March 2026 revision is retitled "Still Waters, Rapid Currents: Early Labor Market Transformation under Generative AI."
- **Key finding:** No significant effect on earnings or hours. The 2025 version's confidence intervals rule out average effects above about 1%; the 2026 revision rules out effects above about 2%. Time savings are about 3%, with weak wage pass-through.
- **Relevance:** This is the strongest null result in the literature, and it covers several female-dominated occupations such as teachers and customer-support workers.

### Otis, Cranney, Delecourt & Koning (2024) — Global Evidence on Gender Gaps and Generative AI
- **Main contribution:** A meta-analysis of the gender gap in generative-AI use.
- **Method:** About 16–18 studies with more than 100,000 people worldwide, including experiments with equal access. HBS working paper.
- **Key finding:** Women use generative AI less than men, even when access is equal.
- **Relevance:** This adds a mechanism. If gains from AI go to users, the gender gap may come through adoption rather than exposure, which your Table 11 design can't see.

### Smith (2026) — Men Are Experiencing More Job Growth Than Women in AI-Exposed Occupations (Pew Research Center)
- **Main contribution:** A descriptive U.S. comparison of employment growth by sex and AI exposure.
- **Method:** CPS, 2017–19 compared with January 2023–June 2026, using O*NET (v30.3) exposure scores across 480 occupations. Short read, not peer-reviewed.
- **Key finding:** Per one-point rise in exposure, men's employment grew 6.3% vs women's 0.2%. Among workers with a high-school education or less, men's employment rose 8.8% while women's fell 9.5%. Among those with some college, men's rose 3.6% while women's fell 6%. For bachelor's degree holders there was no significant change (0.8% vs 0.7%). Pew says the relationship "is not necessarily direct."
- **Relevance:** This is the one generative-AI-era U.S. source that points toward women doing worse in exposed jobs, and it runs against your positive gap. It uses the same CPS data as your IPUMS work but a different exposure measure and pre-period, so reconciling the two is a concrete task for the final paper.

### OECD (2024) — Algorithm and Eve: How AI Will Impact Women at Work
- **Main contribution:** A cross-country policy brief on AI exposure and women's employment.
- **Method:** OECD policy brief drawing on Lane (2024), OECD working paper "Who will be the workers most affected by AI?". Occupational exposure and employment change, 2012–2022, controlling for other technologies, offshorability and trade. Published December 6, 2024 (DOI 10.1787/a1603510-en).
- **Key finding:** Overall exposure is similar for men and women. Women's employment grew faster than men's in highly exposed occupations, partly through entry into male-dominated jobs. Clerical work is highly exposed, with women over-represented, especially those without tertiary education.
- **Relevance:** This is pre-generative-AI evidence that runs against a "women lose" prior, and it matches your positive gap.

### Huang (2024) — The Labor Market Impact of Artificial Intelligence: Evidence from US Regions (IMF WP 2024/199)
- **Main contribution:** Local-labor-market effects of AI adoption.
- **Method:** U.S. commuting zones, 2010–2021.
- **Key finding:** Higher-adoption areas saw larger falls in employment-to-population ratios, and the effect was "more pronounced on men than women."
- **Relevance:** Again, pre-generative-AI evidence that men were hit harder.

### Theory: Autor, Levy & Murnane (2003); Black & Spitz-Oener (2010); Cortes, Jaimovich & Siu (2018)
- **Autor, Levy & Murnane (2003, *QJE*):** computers substitute for routine tasks and complement non-routine ones. This is the base model for why clerical work declines.
- **Black & Spitz-Oener (2010, *REStat* 92(1)):** in West Germany, women's routine task input fell sharply relative to men's, and the shift toward analytic and interactive tasks helps explain wage convergence. Technology reshaped women's work in their favor.
- **Cortes, Jaimovich & Siu (2018, NBER WP 24274):** women gained in high-wage cognitive occupations because social skills became more important there. They explain why technology shocks can favor women within occupations even as women's routine jobs shrink.

## Thematic Organization

### Theoretical Contributions
The task-based framework (Autor, Levy & Murnane) predicts that exposure hurts routine clerical jobs, which are female-dominated. A second strand (Black & Spitz-Oener; Cortes, Jaimovich & Siu) shows that earlier technology waves raised women's relative position by shifting them toward analytic and social tasks. These predictions pull in opposite directions for generative AI, which affects both writing and analysis and clerical work. Manning & Aguirre add a welfare dimension: the same displacement shock costs more for workers with less savings, narrower skills and thinner local markets. That makes exposure × capacity, not exposure alone, the policy-relevant quantity.

### Empirical Findings
- **Exposure by gender (consistent):** ILO 2026, ILO–NASK 2025, Brookings 2026 and the Canaries update all find women more exposed in the U.S. and high-income countries. OECD 2024 is the exception, finding similar overall exposure, but it uses a broader AI measure that isn't specific to LLMs.
- **Young workers (consistent across datasets):** Canaries (ADP), Hosseini & Lichtinger (résumés) and Dominski et al. (CPS) all find declines for young workers in exposed occupations through hiring.
- **Aggregate employment (mostly null):** Yale Budget Lab, EIG, Dominski et al. (all ages) and Humlum & Vestergaard (Denmark).
- **Gender realized outcomes (thin, and mixed in direction):** Pre-generative-AI studies find men hurt more (IMF 2024) or women gaining (OECD 2024). The two generative-AI-era sources differ. The Canaries dashboard finds young women behind at every exposure level but not because of exposure. Pew (2026) finds men gaining more than women as exposure rises, especially without a degree.

### Methodological Innovations
- High-frequency payroll data with firm-time fixed effects (Canaries).
- Adoption identified from job postings (Hosseini & Lichtinger).
- Capability-stage exposure with a pre-period placebo (Dominski et al.).
- A multi-measure exposure sensitivity check (EIG), relevant to your Check 1.
- Adaptive-capacity indices for targeting (Manning & Aguirre).
- Dissimilarity indices against historical technology waves (Yale).

## What Went the Other Way

- **Young-worker decline:** The EIG working paper by Iscenko & Curto Millet (January 2026; both authors at Google) argues the Canaries result is sensitive to hiring inflows and that AI isn't the driver. The Canaries authors acknowledge pre-trends and find smaller gaps once education is accounted for. CPS studies of all ages find no aggregate effect.
- **Women hurt more:** No peer-reviewed generative-AI-era study we found shows women's realized employment falling more *because of* exposure. The direction is contested, though. Pew (2026) finds men's employment grew much more than women's as exposure rises, with women's falling among non-graduates; it is descriptive. The IMF (2024) and OECD (2024) found the opposite before generative AI, and the Canaries dashboard finds a gap unrelated to exposure. Search terms: "AI exposure gender employment outcomes realized women vs men", "generative AI employment effects women female-dominated occupations", "null result".
- **Manning & Aguirre's 86% figure:** No contrary result found. It rests on Lightcast gender shares at the occupation level, and no independent replication was found. Search terms: "Manning 2026 adaptive capacity women clerical 86%".
- **Women more exposed:** The OECD (2024) finds roughly equal overall exposure, with women under-represented in the very highest-exposure jobs. The difference depends on the measure (broad AI vs LLM-specific).

## Gaps and Opportunities

1. **No peer-reviewed generative-AI-era study of realized employment by occupational gender composition in the U.S.** The Canaries dashboard (unpublished) and Pew's descriptive short read are the only ones, and they point in different directions. A Table 11 / IPUMS design like yours, using fixed 2022 gender groups and pre-registered checks, fills this gap directly, and it should explain why its sign differs from Pew's.
2. **Gender and occupation family are confounded.** Female-dominated high-exposure jobs are clerical, and male-dominated ones are mostly tech. No study separates gender composition from occupation family within exposure levels. A within-major-group or matched-occupation design would.
3. **Adaptive capacity hasn't been linked to realized outcomes.** Manning & Aguirre's index is a snapshot. Testing whether low-capacity, high-exposure occupations (mostly women) actually fared worse after 2022 would turn the index from a prediction into evidence.
4. **Policy evaluation.** DOL's Rapid Reskill grants (June 2026) and AI literacy framework haven't been evaluated, and none of them targets women or clerical workers by name. Whether grant priorities reach the 6.1 million high-exposure, low-capacity workers is an open and answerable question.
5. **Adoption gaps vs exposure gaps.** Otis et al. show women use generative AI less. If returns accrue to users, gender effects may come through usage within occupations, which occupation-level data can't see.

- Get the Canaries Dashboard gender series from Stanford DEL / ADP Research and compare it with your HEF/HEM gap for ages 22–25.
